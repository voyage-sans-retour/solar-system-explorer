The Sun

Quick Facts

  • The sun is about 4.6 billion years old. (middle age)
  • It is clasified as a G type star
  • Its surface temperature is about 5,500°C

What makes the Sun shine?

This used to be a long debated question. It has its roots in the ancient idea that the sun has fire within, and has continued to the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. Nineteenth century astronomers came up with a process called Gravitation Contraction, claiming that the sun was shrinking very slightly each year as it converted gravitation potential energy to thermal energy. At the time, this contraction would not have been detectable, and calculations showed that in this way the sun could shine for 25 million years.

In the twentieth century, Einstein published his special theory of relativity, in which he showed that mass itself already has an enormous amount of potential energy. This breakthrough helped scientists discover that the sun uses a process called nuclear fusion to convert mass to energy.

What is the Sun's structure?

Simply put, the sun is a giant ball of hot gas. But a closer look shows us that it is a layered body. The sun is surrounded by an atmosphere, a solar wind of charged particles. It blows back material that later forms tails of comets. Closer to the surface, there is also a low density gas called the corona. There are 2 other layers to the sun's atmosphere called the chromosphere and photosphere.

Inside the sun, there is the convection zone, where energy from the core has risen upward. Then there is the radiation zone, where energy moves as photons of light. Not visible light, but X rays. Lastly, deep within the sun is the solar core, where nuclear fusion takes place.

FUN FACT: The early universe consisted mainly of hydrogen and helium. The complex elements that have made life on earth and our existence possible were made in the cores of stars.

What are sunspots?

The sun is made of an electrically charged gas called plasma. This affects the magnetic field, causing lines that twist as the sun moves and do not allow heat to rise to that a specific spot. This creates the cool, dark regions we know as sunspots.

FUN FACT: The number of sunspots varies as the sun goes through an 11 year cycle, and scientists use them to help track this cycle.
